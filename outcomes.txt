STEP 5.1  ·  LOAD, INSPECT AND CLEAN
Three things in the data needed a decision
441 missing incomes
Mostly Self-Employed and Business Owner applicants (11–13% of each). Fill with the median for that employment type — and note that missing income is itself a self-employed pattern.
1,039 blank credit scores
Not errors. These are new-to-credit applicants — credit_history_months is 0 for every one. Keep them and flag them with is_ntc. Dropping them removes 9% of applicants.
12 incomes above ₹6 lakh a month
All Salaried. Almost certainly annual income entered as monthly. Divide by 12 — or drop them, as long as you say so.
A cleaning log a bank reviewer could agree with is worth as much as the code.

STEP 5.2  ·  WHAT DRIVES APPROVAL TODAY?
Below a credit score of 650, the bank almost never approves
What a strong answer says
Approval jumps from 4.3% to 32.8% across the 650 line. The bank has an unwritten score floor — a rule a pre-screener can apply with confidence.

New-to-credit applicants are approved 53.9% of the time: the credit team is using judgement here.

Gig / Contract workers are approved least (34.9% vs 58.4% for Salaried).
"Lower scores get fewer approvals" describes the chart. "Below 650 the bank almost never approves" is a policy finding.

=== SLIDE 4 ===
STEP 5.3  ·  AFFORDABILITY FEATURES
Once EMIs take more than half of income, approvals collapse
What a strong answer says
FOIR = (existing EMI + proposed EMI) ÷ monthly income.

Approval falls from 65.4% below 30% FOIR to 15.5% above 55%. The sharpest drop is past 50%.

A policy limit of 50–55% FOIR is what the historical decisions support.
FOIR turns out to be one of the three strongest drivers in the whole dataset — and you built it yourself.

The model learned the same policy you found in Steps 5.2 and 5.3
Reading it like a credit analyst
Positive pushes towards decline; negative towards approval.

Higher FOIR, more late payments and being a gig worker push towards decline. A higher credit score and a salaried job pull towards approval.

Because features were scaled, sizes can be compared directly.

=== SLIDE 9 ===
STEP 5.6  ·  FAIRNESS CHECK
The raw gap is real — but it comes mostly from income and job mix
What a strong answer says
Overall: 54.1% male vs 50.4% female approved. Within Gig and Business Owner, the gap is under 1 point.

Female applicants have lower median income (₹45K vs ₹55K) and are almost twice as likely to be gig workers (21% vs 12%).

The model's auto-decline rate is almost identical by gender.
Leaving gender out is necessary, not sufficient. Income and job type can act as stand-ins — monitor outcomes by gender after launch.

=== SLIDE 10 ===
STEP 5.7  ·  CREDIT POLICY BRIEFING
What a distinction-level briefing sounds like
"I recommend auto-declining an application only when the model is at least 90% confident it will be declined. On our test data, that removes about 15.2% of applications — roughly 456 a month, or 190 analyst hours — and fewer than 5% of those would have been approved by the credit team. Two factors drive decisions most: FOIR, where approvals fall from 65.4% below 30% to 15.5% above 55%, and credit score, where approvals are under 5% below 650. The model should not decide on new-to-credit applicants — it is right only 56.7% of the time for them — so they should always go to an analyst. Gender is not used and auto-decline rates are almost identical by gender, but income and job type can act as proxies, so I would report outcomes by gender every month. Before going live, I would want repayment data: today the model copies past analyst decisions, not whether customers actually repaid."
Threshold · applications and hours · two drivers with numbers · a group to keep with humans · fairness position · one more piece of data.

What to carry into your next use case
1
A model's output is a probability. The decision is a threshold — and the business sets it.
2
Translate every result into the stakeholder's units: applications, hours, customers, rupees.
3
Always ask who the model should not decide for — and say so plainly.
